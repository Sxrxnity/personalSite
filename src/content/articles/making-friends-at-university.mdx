This article mostly focuses on introspection (which is kind of ironic, given the title suggests it's about other
people).

It rests on the **first principle** assumption that, to a large extent, the only person we can change in this world is
ourselves.

Please don't treat it as a lesson or a criticism. It's more a reflection on what I think I can do better.

## I: Defining Friendship

For a long time, I didn't bother to define the word 'friend' precisely. As a result, I fell into the common belief that
friends were simply the people I hung around with, studied with and did activities with. I've come to believe that
this phenomenon is better captured by the term 'proximity'.

While proximity is certainly an important element of friendship, it isn't sufficient by itself, at least in the way I
define the word 'friend'. I consider a friend to be someone I can have insightful conversations with in at least one
domain or discipline. Whether or not you agree with this definition is entirely up to you, but keeping it in mind will
help you understand my perspective on making friends at university.

## II: Why It's 'Hard' to Make Friends

Unlike high school, where we would repeatedly interact with the same characters, university reshuffles the people
around us every semester. We get to see our classmates maybe once or twice a week for 14 weeks, and that's it.
If those interactions aren't particularly memorable, the 'friendship' quickly peters out, since proximity was what
kept it going in the first place.

I believe this phenomenon largely holds true for society subcommittees as well. Choosing which subcoms you apply to is
mostly based on 'vibes'. Which people your directors pick is mostly based on 'vibes' as well. As a result of all this
randomness, there are many instances where members of a subcommittee feel isolated (which defeats the purpose of
joining in the first place).

This is not to say that societies are a 'bad' avenue for making friends. If utilised correctly, they (like anything) can
be a great source for finding friends. However, passively expecting friends (as I've defined them) to simply materialise
after you've joined a society is ludicrous. Getting in is not the end; it's the beginning.

## III: So How Then?

How many times (society or not) have you met someone new at uni, only for the conversation to go something like
this: "What's your name? What year are you in? What degree do you study? What societies are you in? What courses are
you doing?"

This gets repetitive and boring fast, so why do we keep coming back to these questions? I think it's largely because
most people don't have anything 'interesting' to talk about. If someone asked you to name all the interesting and
meaningful activities you've done in the past week, month, or year, what would you say? If the list is limited to "work,
study, hanging out with friends, going to concerts", or similar activities, there's only so much you can talk about.

On the other hand, if you're genuinely passionate about something (I cannot **stress** enough how much weight those two
words carry), and have invested substantial effort into it, that will inherently be interesting. The **order of
operations** remains 'have something interesting to talk about' → 'talk about something interesting', not the inverse.

In short, do genuinely interesting things with your life. Not what the majority deems to be interesting, but what
**you** deem to be interesting. In doing so, you'll often find others exploring similar avenues, and your shared passion
for whatever discipline it is will naturally foster mutual respect and interest. Even if you don't find many people, at
least you'll be able to keep yourself company, and that's not something most people can do.

The first and most important person you should become friends with is yourself. If you've learnt how to do that,
interacting with everyone else becomes a lot easier.

## IV: Imprecisions

There are many factors which this article does not consider when it comes to making friends at university. There are
certainly contextual factors which influence (both positively and negatively) one's ability to make friends, and I
wouldn't disagree if you pointed out that this article doesn't address them.

However, as stated in the introduction, the focus is on what we can change. If we want to make friends, it is **our**
responsibility, and no one else's, to try to make that happen. Blaming the environment (no matter how true it may be) is
ultimately fruitless.
