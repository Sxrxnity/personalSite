## I: What Does Effective Communication Entail?

**First principles** - The purpose of communication (for the speaker) is to deliver 'something' to the receiver. If the
receiver successfully understands that 'something', that instance of communication was successful.

It's easy to visualise this if we imagine two people, one of whom speaks English and the other Spanish, trying to
compliment each other. They could be saying the nicest things in the world, but because neither can comprehend what the
other wants to express, their communication is not effective.

Ineffective communication becomes much harder to recognise when both parties speak the same 'language'. However, due to
a number of contextual factors such as culture and upbringing, the same language often differs in what its speakers
intend to convey with a particular word.

"Clean" to someone who was brought up on a farm might be vastly different from "clean" to someone who lives in a mansion
maintained by butlers. (Excuse the generalisations, but hopefully you get the sentiment I intend to express.)

The fact is that everyone speaking, say, English has their own shade, or perception, of what each word means. Most
people tend to believe that their perception is reality (sometimes consciously, sometimes subconsciously). This is not
inherently a flaw; in fact, it's how we as humans make sense of the world around us without having all the knowledge
required to fill in every single gap.

However, this is not good for effective communication, as it means we give a little too much importance to our own
perception, and a little less to others' perception. The "right" balance (if there even is one) of different
perceptions is much more of an art than it is a science, but it is a limit that we, who strive to be effective
communicators, must persistently reach for.

what we wish to communicate, we must align ourselves with their language.

On the rare occasion you meet someone who is also a competent speaker and receiver, you'll find communication much
easier. You'll both be actively adapting your language to the other's, while translating theirs into your own.

## II: Humility and Sensitivity

Recognising that effective communication is more complex than most people perceive it to be is only half the battle;
putting it into practice requires great humility and sensitivity.

A strong and accurate sense of what someone means when they speak certain words is hard to come by, and requires much
practice and reflection. Oftentimes, you'll find that things people have previously said to you make more sense once
you've been on the other side of the tunnel.

**You truly feel what they mean only after you've experienced it yourself.**

I think, in general, sensitivity and depth of perception can be acquired through breadth of experience and a decent
amount of reflection. Humility, true humility, is much harder to come by.

For one, sensitivity, once acquired, does not require persistent effort, in the sense that it's more of a 'technical' skill. It's like
learning to solve algebraic equations: once you learn the techniques, you don't really forget them.

Humility, on the other hand, is more of an 'attitude' thing. Coming back to the maths analogy, humility is acknowledging
that you can't yet solve every equation, and genuinely wanting to learn how.

Recently, I've come to realise that there are at least two different levels of humility. The first is logical
understanding, i.e. one knows all the benefits of being humble and chooses to be so, yet at one's core there is still a
desire to prove something to others. The second is what I define as 'true' humility, a state even harder to reach than
the first.

There's a famous saying that goes: "The more you know, the more you realise how little you know." Well, it takes getting
to a certain level to:

<ol type="a">
  <li>acquire enough expertise in a domain to realise all the facets in which you can improve</li>
  <li>not care about how most people perceive your skill, as you are more than aware of your calibre</li>
  <li><strong>genuinely feel</strong> that candidly listening to everyone's perspective (no matter how far their true
    skill pales in comparison to yours) presents you with potential guidance to make monumental improvements</li>
</ol>

I cannot overstate how much one needs to excel in a certain discipline to reach that state. With that being said, I think it's
much healthier to think of humility as a continuous spectrum, as opposed to two discrete values. Much like anything
complex in this world, it is not black and white; there's a lot more granularity than first meets the eye.

From personal experience, I've found that I grow the most when I'm able to put down my desire to prove and genuinely
try to listen to the other person. Even when what they say doesn't bear directly on the matter at hand, it often
offers insight, or at least food for thought, in some other area.

**Not everyone can be a great teacher, but I can always be a good student.**

## III: Evaluating People

I would like to invoke two points alluded to in previous sections:

1. People tend to overcredit their own perception and undercredit others'.
2. Much communication involves misunderstanding, which often doesn't even get recognised.

As a result, people often misjudge each other, and it's especially tragic when, from a third-party perspective, I can
see that both parties mean well.

Things go horribly wrong when one party takes a single misunderstanding and extrapolates the other's entire
personality from it. Having been on both ends of this myself, I've seen those extrapolations prove misguided, if not
outright untrue, far more often than not.

In general, we'd come to a much more accurate understanding of another human being by:

<ol type="a">
  <li>considering the context in which they grew up, i.e. their childhood, upbringing, and any significant emotional
    events that would have shaped their psyche or character</li>
  <li>trying to understand their 'language', instead of just interpreting their words through our own</li>
  <li>giving people more than one chance to show who they are</li>
</ol>

These are idealistic, for sure, and oftentimes we won't have the time or energy to do all three. Still, for our own
sake, we should persistently strive for the best.
