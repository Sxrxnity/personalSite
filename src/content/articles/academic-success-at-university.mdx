I've been at uni for a decent amount of time now, and I'm still maintaining a High Distinction WAM (Weighted Average
Mark).

As a UNSW student, my insight will largely be based on my experiences at this particular university, though I'm sure
you'll be able to extrapolate bits that are useful to you if you attend a different institution.

<Toc headings={props.headings} />

## I: Macro Considerations

If you haven't read the article on [HSC and Tutoring](/articles/hsc-and-tutoring/), I recommend skimming through it
first. A lot of the principles I reference here were established there.

In short, find your purpose, build 'soft' skills, and everything afterward will come a lot easier.

## II: Course Selection

Most people do not spend nearly enough time on course selection. This is like laying the foundation before you begin
building. The firmer this is, the easier your term will be, especially in the later stages.

Start by laying out what you want from your courses. Light workload? Overlapping classes with friends? All the
requirements you want a great timetable to satisfy. Then make a list based on priority.

Starting with the highest priority goal, research at least 3 courses that would allow you to reach that goal. For
example, if your goal was to obtain a high WAM, you might look for courses (coming from a 'maths' kid perspective) that
have more lab tests and midterms as opposed to written and portfolio assignments. Use the publicly available course
outline finder tool to learn everything you can about your prospective courses.

At the beginning of your university career, finding courses should be fairly easy. Every degree (more or less) will
have core subjects that you will need to do, so there's not much choice to be had. As you progress through each term,
please make a habit of proactively planning your courses. The few extra hours you spend picking better class times and
assessments that suit your skillset more will be handsomely rewarded throughout the term.

## III: Lectures

This is probably the most misunderstood component of university teaching. If I had a dollar for every time someone
told me how they're X lectures behind or how they'll just watch it on 2 times speed later, I would have enough to open
my own university.

**First principles** - Lectures are meant to teach you the theoretical content for that course. If you can obtain that
knowledge using other methods, the lectures are unnecessary.

Be honest with yourself. How many times have you "watched" a lecture, only to remember basically nothing after 5
minutes, an hour, or a day? How many times have you taken "notes", only to never read them again? Please stop
convincing yourself that you're studying when you're just going through the motions.

Find a method that genuinely gets you engaged with the content. For me, that's feeding an LLM the lecture slides, then
asking it to build a story and explain the contents of the lecture to me like I was 5. I prefer to get first a
structural grasp of what the entire lecture is about, and the motivations behind why I should care about this content
(aside from it being on the exam). Then I will go through the slides myself, and ask the LLM any technical questions I
may have. If I don't understand why certain details are relevant, I will ask for their purpose.

Once I understood the first principles behind why the content was relevant for a course, intuition could fill in a lot
of the gaps.

**You would be surprised how much easier learning is when you're doing it proactively.**

## IV: Tutorials/Labs

**First principles** - these classes are meant to help you develop a deeper understanding of the lecture content by
giving you a chance to apply it in practical scenarios.

If you can obtain a level of understanding sufficient for your exams (assuming this is your primary objective), there
is little purpose for attending these classes (unless there is a participation/attendance component).

Please, please, please do not take this as an excuse to skip your classes. The point I am trying to make is simply that
learning is an active process. Find the avenues that get you actively engaged. In an ideal world, you would not only be
going to all your classes, but also engaging with your professors and peers in discussion afterward.

In an ideal world, all of us would be genuinely invested in whatever disciplines we're studying, because genuine
interest and love for what you do is the **only** way to reach the top of your field.

The unfortunate reality is that most of us are only at university because we feel we have to be, not because we want
to be.

It was never a proactive decision.

## V: Assessments

As soon as you can obtain course outlines (generally start of Week 1), save copies. Figure out as much as you can
regarding when your assessments are. Put the prospective deadlines in your calendar so you have a clear idea of what
needs to be done when.

For me, I apply this not just to assessments, but to weekly work as well. I like to get all my mandatory weekly work
started the day it is released. I never leave things until the last minute.

I aim to always be on top of my work, not the other way around.

In short, obtain clarity early. Start things early. If you've found your purpose (or at least partially), the soft
skills necessary to do these simple tasks should feel a lot easier to come by.

For final exams in general, start preparing a few weeks in advance. If you feel as if you can achieve a satisfactory
result in less time, you can always dial down the time you devote to that subject. You can't create more time though,
so start earlier.

If your pace is what dictates when you get things done, then you're in a good spot. If the deadlines dictate your pace,
you're in trouble.

**Be proactive.**
