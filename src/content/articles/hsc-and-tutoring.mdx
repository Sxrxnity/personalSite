If you're in your senior years of high school, this article is for you.

If you're planning to pursue a degree that requires an ATAR of 99.7+, this article will probably not be enough, but
hopefully it will give you what you need to get ~99.5. As I have not personally reached the pinnacle of HSC success, I
cannot confidently articulate the minute differences that separate a 99.95 and a 99.90. Differences at the highest level
are extremely small; I do not understand what that takes.

This article is split into several sections; read only the ones you find interesting:

## I: Don't Listen to Anyone

Treat the title artistically, i.e. don't take it literally. It's only true in the sense that you should think critically
instead of blindly believing what anyone (including me) says.

I've never been a fan of tutoring centres using past students' achievements to persuade future students to join.
There's nothing evil about this strategy; it's just logically flawed. Being a <u>good test-taker</u> is not equivalent
to being a <u>good student</u>, which is also not equivalent to being a <u>good teacher</u>. These are 3 completely
different concepts.

The HSC only tests the first one. A good tutor should be the second and third, but most of the time they're just the
first.

Most people are not good test-takers, let alone good students. This shows most clearly when students transition from
high school to university. Without the relentless force of 'Asian' parents (in the sense that they hyperfocus on
academic achievement) pushing them, most stop studying, because they never wanted to.

**First principles** - if you don't want to do something and nothing forces you to, you won't. In high school, the
force your parents, peers, and environment exerted simply outweighed your reluctance to study.

**It's physics.**

I say all of this not to find fault, but simply to illustrate the reality. I see myself only as a decent test-taker and
student. There are infinitely many people who are better at both than I am. If you believe my premises or logical
justifications are invalid, please tell me.

*I will always hold my beliefs firm yet be more than happy to be proven wrong (these are not contradictory).*

That being said, empirical evidence is still meaningful for establishing credibility, so here are some of my accolades
from my time at Baulkham Hills High School:

<div class="highlight">

- 99.45 ATAR
- 13 Band 6 Units
- HSC All Rounder
- 2x State Ranks
- 3x 1st in Course during Year 12
- Music Performance Encore nomination

</div>

Also keep in mind this was done on an average of 3.4 hours of study per day in Year 12, and I never attended tutoring
throughout high school\*. I still attended Cadets every week (the only Year 12 to do so) as the unit's Cadet Training
Officer, participated in badminton and basketball competitions, Scouting, etc.

**I did not study that hard. I studied effectively.**

## II: HSC - First Principles

Why was I able to study effectively? Because I understood what the HSC was about, so most of my efforts never went to
waste.

**First principles** - The HSC is about comparison. There is no fixed aptitude bar, i.e. you need to be better than
your peers.

How do you do that? By doing what they don't / can't.

**Success is simple. Not easy. Simple.**

Be disciplined, because most people lack discipline. Manage your time effectively, because most people don't manage
their time well. Build sustainable habits, because most people are spontaneous. I'll cover how to implement these broad
concepts in more technical detail in [section IV](#iv-what-actionable-steps-can-you-take), but for now I really want to
drive home that it's the 'soft' skills (in the sense that they're not related to any particular subject, but how you
approach studying) that most people lack, making it the easiest place for you to gain an edge.

I assure you I was not even close to being the person who studied the most, but I can confidently say my 'soft' skills
were better than at least 99% of my peers'. I don't believe I am fundamentally better at these things than them. I
simply think they overlooked/underestimated their importance.

**First principles** - Soft skills come so much easier when you have purpose. We only have so much willpower each day.
It's impossible to force ourselves to always do the logical thing, because emotions and feelings have been and always
will be a stronger force. (This is not a bad thing, it's what makes us human)

Just as nobody needs to force you to watch a show you love, you'll naturally be drawn to studying if the right
motivation is in place. Mine was a 99+ ATAR. I wanted to prove to myself that I could compete with everyone around me
who went to tutoring, so I set that number as my goal.

I estimated my ATAR many times throughout year 12 (using HSCNinja), and once I was confident I'd comfortably surpass my
goal, I stopped trying as hard. **Physics.**

<div class="highlight callout">

Without substantial introspection, you will not be able to find sound motivation. Without sound motivation, you will
struggle with your soft skills. Without these skills, your performance will be substantially impacted.

Most of the time, when we run into serious problems, we look to external reasons. However, this is very unlikely to
yield any meaningful insight. The external environment around you is largely a product of who you are internally.
Messiness, procrastination, apathy; these are not things that can be resolved when you look outward.

There is no way for me to give you purpose. Your life is yours, and yours only. However, if you can find why **you**
want to excel in the HSC, I guarantee you'll improve in a manner that you previously thought was impossible.

</div>

## III: Where Tutoring Falls Short

**Tutoring cannot give you purpose.**

In its current form, it fails to foster the self-discovery needed to build soft skills. What about hard skills then?

Pre-2023, I would've conceded that tutoring was one of the most effective solutions to combat the discrepancy in
teaching quality at different high schools. However, LLMs like ChatGPT and Claude have since completely changed the
narrative. For a much, much cheaper price, you have a personal assistant whose knowledge base is infinitely greater than
any human, who can help you understand concepts and generate practice questions 24/7. The little allure tutoring had can
mostly be replaced with a much cheaper and more effective alternative.

Some of you still might see value in physically keeping students in a learning environment as opposed to rotting in
bed, but I would argue that since learning is an active process, very little is gained by forcing the physical body to
be in a classroom while the mind is anything but engaged.

Some of you might also argue that tutoring goes into more depth than school, and I would agree with your premise.
However, I disagree with the conclusion that this makes tutoring a worthy investment. Firstly, LLMs offer much greater
depth. Secondly, this depth is largely unnecessary for the HSC.

**First principles** - If we are studying for tests, then we should only concern ourselves with content that is closely
related to the exams.

In short, tutoring cannot offer you soft skills (that requires mentorship, meta-cognition, humility). The hard skills
that it can offer you have much cheaper and more cost-effective alternatives. The only thing tutoring uniquely offers is
a period of time to physically force students to stay in a learning environment (or at least one they perceive to be).
However, if it comes down to that, you're probably not going to do well anyway.

On average, parents probably spend at least $20,000 on a child's tuition fees over the course of their high school
career (a very conservative estimate). This figure could rise to as high as $60,000. This cost already rivals that of a
full 4-year university degree. If your parents' goal (as illogical as it is) is for you to obtain a high ATAR to get
into a "good" degree with promises of high salaries once you find a job, why spend so much unnecessary capital on this
investment?

## IV: What Actionable Steps Can You Take?

So if tutoring can't give you what you need to succeed, what can?

Finding your purpose is definitely important, but treat it as an ongoing journey that happens parallel to your studies
as opposed to a necessary condition you need to fulfil before you begin your studies. Action should coexist with
thought, not one before the other.

The main thing I would try to obtain is clarity.

**First principles** - The primary reason for obtaining a high ATAR is to meet the requirements for a certain degree.
If I can comfortably achieve that requirement, any study beyond that is unnecessary.

With that in mind, I would take the following steps:

1. Research what ATAR requirement you need to get into your course (or just set a target).
2. Enter your subjects into an ATAR calculator (e.g. <a href="https://www.hscninja.com/atar-calculator" target="_blank"
   rel="noopener noreferrer">HSCNinja's ATAR calculator</a>) and play around with different marks. Come up with several
   possible options for reaching your desired ATAR with your subjects. Always estimate conservatively.
3. Assess where you are right now based on any past exams or practice papers.

This should help you obtain a much clearer idea of how far you still need to go. If the gap is big, you will feel it.

In terms of actually studying for subjects, I cannot offer a one-size-fits-all solution.

**First principles** - If it works for you, you should do it.

Although I can't give you a definitive answer, I can offer tools and resources that I have found to be useful:

Tools aside, **please track everything.** Track how much time you spend each day as accurately as possible. Track how
much time you spend on each subject. Track your progress using the traffic light system. You MUST have quantifiable
metrics that can clearly show your progress. Ambiguity and the vague "feeling" that you're making progress is extremely
harmful to your progression.

I have also looked at <a href="https://eliteachievers.com.au/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Elite
Achievers</a> by Jed McDonald-Singh, someone who performed exceptionally well in the HSC despite not going to tutoring.
I subscribed to his platform (post HSC) and went through his core modules. Though it is by no means perfect, it is the
closest I have come to finding a system like the one I used during my HSC journey.

This is NOT an endorsement, but in my personal opinion, his platform is a much better alternative to tutoring. It's
rooted in simplicity, measurable targets, and accountability.
